Fuel & recovery

Eat to grow

Tall, lean and training five days a week plus sport, you burn a lot. Most lean teens who think they “can’t gain” simply aren’t eating enough. The gym gives the signal; food and sleep build the muscle.

Gain rate+0.25–0.5 kgper week
Energy≈3,200–3,800kcal a day, roughly
Protein110–130 ga day, over 4–5 meals
Sleep8–10 hevery night

Use the scale, not a calculator

You don’t need to count calories. Weigh yourself 2–3 mornings a week, after the bathroom and before eating, and log the average on Sunday.

  • Flat for 2 weeks: add 300–500 kcal a day. A glass of whole milk and a peanut-butter sandwich does it.
  • Up 0.25–0.5 kg a week: perfect. Keep going.
  • Up more than 0.5 kg a week for a month: trim the extras a little.

Build every meal the same way

Protein
A palm or two: eggs, chicken, fish, beef, Greek yogurt, milk, paneer or cottage cheese, tofu, lentils, beans.
Carbs
One or two fists: rice, potatoes, pasta, bread, wraps or roti, oats, fruit. Your fuel for training and sport.
Fats
A thumb or two: olive oil, nuts, peanut butter, cheese, avocado. The easiest extra calories.
Colour
Fruit or veg at most meals.

Timing and drinks

  • Eat 1–3 hours before training: carbs plus a little protein. Never train on empty after school; keep a snack in your bag.
  • Have a proper meal within about 2 hours after.
  • Drink enough that your pee is pale yellow. More on hot days and sport days. Milk counts.
  • Your bones are still building: get calcium daily (milk, yogurt, cheese or fortified alternatives) and some daylight for vitamin D.

Supplements

  • You don’t need any. Food first, always.
  • Protein powder is just convenient food. Fine if it helps you hit your protein.
  • Creatine is well studied in adults, but most youth sports-medicine groups advise waiting until 18 unless a doctor says otherwise.
  • Skip pre-workouts and energy drinks (lots of caffeine), fat burners and “test boosters”.
  • Never touch steroids or SARMs. At 15 they can shut down your own hormone production and stop you growing taller. Your natural hormones are about to do this job for free.

Sleep is training

  • 8–10 hours. Teens need more sleep than adults, and deep sleep is when most growth and muscle repair happens.
  • Same bedtime and wake time every day, within about an hour at weekends.
  • Phone out of bed 30–60 minutes before sleep. Cool, dark room.
  • No caffeine after about 2 pm. A 20–30 minute nap is fine.
  • Teen athletes who sleep under 8 hours get injured noticeably more often. Sleep is part of the plan, not a break from it.

Guide

How it works

Why you feel the way you do, how to run the plan, and how to keep your back healthy for life.

Why you don’t feel athletic yet

  • You grew faster than your coordination. Bones grow first. Muscles, tendons and your brain’s map of your body catch up later, so tall teens often feel clumsy for a while. Sprinting, jumping and crawling practice speeds up the catch-up.
  • Long levers, not much muscle yet. If you’re tall and lean, you have long arms and legs and not much muscle moving them yet. Every sprint, jump and pull-up costs you more than it costs a shorter, heavier friend. Getting stronger and gaining muscle changes this more than anything else.
  • You stopped playing on the floor. Kids crawl, climb, roll and squat all day. Teens mostly sit. Movement is a skill you keep by using it. The Ground Game warm-ups and Sunday’s flow bring it back.
  • Sport isn’t endurance training. Most team sports are stop-start. Without easy aerobic work your base stays small, so you gas early. Under-eating makes it worse.

The 12 weeks

Weeks 1–4Foundation

Learn the lifts and own the positions. Slow lowering, sprints at 85–90%.

Weeks 5–8Build

Heavier, more sets, all-out sprints. Size and strength start to move.

Weeks 9–12Athlete

Heaviest lifts, jumps paired with heavy sets, flying sprints. Then retest.

Weeks 4 and 8 are lighter: about half the sets and 10% less weight, so your body can catch up. Week 12 is light too, with retesting. Tests happen in weeks 1, 4, 8 and 12.

Rules for every session

Effort
RIR means reps in reserve: RIR 2 = stop with 2 good reps left. Never grind ugly reps on the main lifts, and skip 1-rep-max testing. You don’t need it.
Order
Warm-up, speed and power, main lift, supersets, core, cardio. Fast work goes first because it’s useless when you’re tired.
Rest
Sprints and jumps: full rest. Main lifts: 2–3 min. Supersets: 60–90 s between exercises.
Progress
Main lifts: when every set hits the target at RIR 2 or more, add 2.5–5 kg to leg lifts or 1–2.5 kg to upper-body lifts the next week. Rep ranges like 8–12: add reps until every set hits the top, then add weight and start again at the bottom.
Form
Film your main lifts from the side and compare with the cues. Ask a PE teacher, school strength coach or qualified trainer to check your deadlift and squat early on.
Short on time
Keep the warm-up, the power work, the main lift and Superset A. Drop the rest.
Missed a day
Don’t double up. Do the next session, or slide the missed one to your next free day.
Colours
Block colours follow competition weight plates: red 25 kg for speed and power, blue 20 for strength, yellow 15 for muscle, green 10 for engine, white 5 for ground work and back armor.

Back armor: future-proofing your spine

Your spine isn’t fragile. It gets stronger with gradual, well-done loading, like the rest of you, and regular exercise is the best-proven way to prevent back pain. If back problems run in your family, these habits matter even more:

  • Trunk endurance. The McGill Big 3 (curl-up, side plank, bird dog) most days. For backs, endurance matters more than max strength.
  • Hinge with your hips. Trap-bar deadlifts and RDLs teach your hips to do the work while your spine stays neutral.
  • Strong glutes and hamstrings take load off your lower back.
  • Mobile hips and upper back, so your lower back doesn’t bend to make up for them.
  • Resist movement. Pallof presses resist twisting, dead bugs and rollouts resist arching, carries and side planks resist leaning.
  • No ego lifting, and no heavy rounded-back lifting when you’re tired.
  • Keep it even. Left and right side planks within about 10% of each other.
  • Move often. No single posture is bad; staying in one for hours is. Get up every 30–45 minutes.

Looking athletic

  • Shoulders and back first. Lateral raises, pull-ups, rows and face pulls widen your shoulders and make your waist look smaller.
  • Upper chest and arms fill out a T-shirt.
  • Legs and glutes. Every athlete has them, and they drive your speed.
  • Own your height. Tall teens often slouch. A strong upper back and standing tall make you look bigger instantly.
  • Gain slowly. 0.25–0.5 kg a week keeps the gain mostly muscle.

You won’t end up looking like a bodybuilder. You train for speed and power too, with athletic movements and moderate volume, which builds muscle that works.

Sport and game days

  • Sport comes first when it clashes with the gym.
  • Day before a match: no heavy legs or sprints. Do an upper session or just Daily Armor.
  • Day after a hard match: do Sunday’s session instead.
  • Keep heavy leg days at least 48 hours before games.
  • Busy season with 2+ games a week: drop Saturday’s intervals and Sunday’s long cardio (your sport covers it), and do about two-thirds of the sets on Monday, Wednesday and Friday.
  • Practice and gym on the same day: lift after practice, or leave a few hours between.

Doing too much? Sore for 3+ days, sleeping badly, getting slower or weaker, no appetite, short-tempered. Take 2–3 easy days, or run a lighter week early.

Soreness or pain?

Soreness: normal

A dull ache in the muscle, usually on both sides, that fades in 1–3 days. Warm up well and train.

Pain: stop that exercise

Sharp, in a joint or bone, on one side, getting worse, or changing how you move. Use the swap, and get it checked if it lasts more than a week.

See a doctor or physio if

  • Lower-back pain lasts more than 2 weeks, especially if leaning backward hurts. Stress fractures in the lower back (spondylolysis) are common in teen athletes and need rest, not training through.
  • Back pain comes with numbness, tingling or weakness in a leg, or wakes you at night.
  • You get pain just below the kneecap or at the back of the heel during a growth spurt. Growth-plate irritation (Osgood-Schlatter, Sever’s) needs less jumping for a while.
  • You wheeze, cough or get a tight chest during or after exercise. Exercise-induced asthma is common, very treatable, and could be part of why you gas out.
  • You feel wiped out all the time despite eating and sleeping well.

If back problems run in your family, it’s also worth asking your doctor for a quick spine check at your next visit.

What to expect

  1. Weeks 1–4

    Moving feels smoother and less stiff. Strength climbs fast as your nervous system learns the lifts. Easy cardio stops feeling awful.

  2. Weeks 5–8

    Shoulders, back and arms start to look different if you’re eating enough. Sprint and jump numbers move. Games feel easier.

  3. Weeks 9–12

    About 3–5 kg heavier, clearly stronger, quicker off the mark. Retest and compare with week 1.

  4. After that

    Run the 12 weeks again with heavier weights, or progress the lifts: goblet to front or back squat, trap bar to straight-bar deadlift (coached), landmine to standing overhead press. Keep the warm-ups, Sundays and Daily Armor for good. The big changes come from stacking blocks like this for a year or two, and the next few years are your best window for building muscle.

General training guidance, not medical advice. If you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, get checked first, ideally by a physio or sports doctor who works with young athletes. Let a parent or coach know what you’re doing.
